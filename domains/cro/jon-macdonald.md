## Beliefs
- "Conversion rate optimization just doesn't cut it anymore" — classic CRO treats symptoms (the conversion moment) rather than underlying conditions; the whole journey must be optimized or you merely get more people to a conversion stage that is itself broken [2]
- Optimization is about understanding human nature and inherent biases, not tactics: "optimization tactics and technology trends can only get you so far" [4]
- Discounts train discount consumers — a 20%-off popup signals "our products aren't worth full price" and is nearly impossible to walk back [5]
- Conversion is continuous listening, iterating and testing; there is no silver bullet [4]

## Principles
- Customer-first: know who they are, what they want, how best to serve it — websites that save customers time earn more money [1]
- Add value, don't subtract price: offers (free gift, free expedited shipping, BOGO) instead of % discounts [5]
- Navigation and messaging should be about the consumer's problem, not the brand ("about you" belongs in the footer) [5]
- Don't send visitors back up the funnel: awareness content (recipes, blog) belongs as supporting content on product pages, not the homepage [5]

## Frameworks
- **Digital Experience Optimization™ (DXO)** (2024, replaced Conversion Growth Program): research + testing + implementation across the full customer journey, not just conversion points; "diagnose what is getting users stuck, find solutions to improve the digital journey" [2,3]
- **5-Factors Scorecard™**: 18-question program assessment producing an action plan [3]
- **A/B vs rapid-testing decision framework** (published 2025): choose validation method by strategic importance × risk [6]
  - Use A/B testing when: ≥1,000 visitors/week to the page, change is reversible, stakes justify time, technical capability exists (homepage redesigns, pricing pages, signup flows, onboarding, high-traffic landing pages)
  - Use rapid testing (wireframe/mockup concept tests, 6-8 variations, qualitative) when: traffic too low, technical limits, time pressure (feature naming, dashboard nav restructuring, enterprise sales pages, value-prop clarity)

## Processes
- Research → diagnosis of what blocks users → test or implement → iterate (the DXO loop) [2,3]
- Teardown practice: fresh perspective on a site, call out issues from a decade+ of experience, prioritized by what the consumer experiences [5]

## Heuristics
- "The first 10 seconds" decide whether a visitor understands what you sell and whether you can solve their pain [5]
- One message per hero; carousels = marketing teams failing to prioritize [5]
- If you can't A/B test it (traffic), validate cheaply with rapid tests or ship reversibly [6]

## Tactics
- Surface product categories in main nav, not brand/about pages [5]
- Replace discount popups with add-on offers; keep upsells at the product category level [5]
- Test both copy and layout; use psychological principles (loss aversion, social proof, choice architecture) from *Behind the Click* [4]

## Tools
- Standard A/B testing platforms; rapid testing tools for concept validation; analytics [6]

## Inputs
- Traffic volume estimate (1K+/week gate for A/B), reversibility assessment, stakes/risk assessment, technical capability [6]
- Customer research (who they are, what they want) before any design [1]

## Outputs
- Teardown reports, prioritized test roadmaps, DXO program recommendations, books (Stop Marketing Start Selling; Opting In To Optimization; Behind the Click, 2026) [1,3,4]

## Metrics
- Conversion rate and revenue (claims average client revenue increase of 100%+; 16:1 ROI on programs; "hundreds of millions in additional revenue") [1,3]

## Decision rules
- A/B test only when traffic ≥1,000 visitors/week AND change is reversible AND stakes are high enough to wait [6]
- If traffic is insufficient → rapid-test the concept (wireframes, 6-8 variations) or make evidence-based reversible changes [6]
- When in doubt between testing and shipping: test high-stakes irreversible changes; ship low-risk reversible ones (footer layouts, minor colors, low-traffic secondary pages) [6]
- Never start a program with a discount; add offers instead [5]

## Failure modes
- Optimizing only the conversion moment (treating symptoms) — the CGP-era trap he publicly retired [2]
- Discount-first strategy: creates discount consumers, destroys price integrity, "extremely hard to get out of the game of discounting once you're in it" [5]
- Testing everything (bottlenecks, slows innovation) or testing nothing (intuition-based changes) — both are failure modes his framework targets [6]
- Brand-focused navigation and hero messages that fail the consumer test in the first 10 seconds [5]

## Contrarian beliefs
- CRO as a discipline name is too narrow; DXO reframes optimization as journey-wide [2]
- Email popups "work" for email collection but hurt brand value and train discount behavior — he'd eliminate them [5]

## Examples
- Primal Kitchen teardown (2022): discount popup critique, nav restructuring, recipe-content repositioning [5]
- Claimed client results: average revenue increase over 100%; 16:1 ROI (agency claim, not independently verified) [1,3]

## Conditions
- DXO fits orgs with enough maturity to run research + testing + implementation continuously; the A/B-vs-rapid framework assumes a team that can actually implement [2,6]
- Teardown heuristics are DTC/ecommerce-leaning (subscription examples: ButcherBox) [5]

## Limitations
- His ROI claims are agency-reported, not published evidence [1,3]
- Rapid testing gives qualitative signal, not causal proof — he treats it as a low-traffic substitute, with before/after measurement as honest fallback (documented in his 2025 framework) [6]
