## Beliefs
- Category design is a strategy of changing the buyer's evaluation criteria, not a brand or launch exercise (Lochhead/Play Bigger) (T2).
- Categories are won by the company that names the problem and leads the ecosystem — category kings capture outsized economics (~76% of category profit, Geroski research via Play Bigger) (T3 — thin/self-referential evidence).
- Skeptic position (Kellogg): categories emerge from a "rugby scrum" of competitors, customers, and analysts; "category design is mostly bunk" — you cannot own what you cannot influence (T1, Kellogg is the named skeptic).
- Moore's middle path: lifecycle of define → develop → dominate — influence over an emergent process rather than creation (T1).

## Principles
- The category is defined by the problem it names, not the product: name the problem in the buyer's terms so the old category's solutions visibly fail it (positioning.md consensus) (T1/T2).
- A category only exists when others use the name: competitors, analysts, media, buyers — it is an adoption campaign, not a launch campaign (existing skill; Lochhead) (T2).
- Category creation is expensive and rare: the burden of teaching the market falls mostly on the creator; only works with sustained budget and CEO evangelism (Lochhead preconditions; Kellogg) (T1/T2).
- "Better on existing criteria is positioning; new criteria is category creation" (existing skill heuristic) (T2).

## Frameworks
- Play Bigger category design process: lightning strikes (thought leadership), POV, category name, ecosystem mobilization (T2).
- Moore's technology adoption lifecycle: beachhead + whole product before broad messaging; pragmatists buy references (T1).
- Kellogg's skepticism gate: can you influence the rugby scrum? If not, category design is fiction (T1).
- Ramadan/Play Bigger economics: category kings ≈76% of profits — treat as practitioner heuristic, not evidence (T3).

## Processes
1. Gate: whitespace (problem buyers can't name) + visibly different solution + budget for multi-year education + CEO willingness (existing skill; Lochhead) (T2).
2. Read the industry first: mature category with entrenched criteria → category design; emerging category → fast positioning inside it (existing skill) (T2).
3. Frame the problem: buyer's terms, old-way mechanism contrast, trigger moment, POV statement; test with real buyers before investing (existing skill; Raskin-adjacent) (T2).
4. Name the category: functional/evocative/compound options (5-10), test "we need a [name] solution", adoption potential over brand-team preference (existing skill) (T2).
5. Mobilize the ecosystem: POV → early adopters → analysts → media → competitors adopting the name; invite second/third players in (existing skill; Lochhead) (T2).
6. Run thought leadership as the engine: problem-level content ladder, evidence engine, language adoption across customer-facing teams (existing skill) (T2).
7. Scoreboard: name adoption, share of category discussion, POV traction — over years, not quarters (existing skill) (T2).

## Heuristics
- A category of one is a fantasy — competitors validating the category grow the pie; stay the leader, not the only player (existing skill) (T2).
- Whoever writes the buying criteria (RFP questions, evaluation frameworks) owns the category (existing skill) (T2).
- Category design without funding/authority to mobilize is fiction (Lochhead preconditions; Kellogg) (T1/T2).

## Inputs
Product context, market map, industry analysis (lifecycle stage), budget/patience assessment, CEO commitment, analyst/partner landscape.

## Outputs
Category strategy, name candidates with adoption scoring, problem frame + POV, ecosystem mobilization plan with adoption metrics, thought-leadership engine plan.

## Metrics
Name adoption (who else uses the name), share of category discussion, analyst category recognition, POV traction, language adoption inside the company; pipeline is a lagging scoreboard, not the goal.

## Decision rules
- IF buyers already have a name for the problem AND competitors share it THEN you are positioning, not designing a category (Kellogg, T1).
- IF the existing category is merely crowded THEN reposition inside it; category design budget is wasted (Kellogg, T1).
- IF the company cannot influence the ecosystem (no budget, no CEO evangelism, no analyst relationships) THEN abandon category design (Kellogg gate + Lochhead preconditions, T2).
- IF the solution is better on existing criteria only THEN it is positioning, not category creation (existing skill, T2).
- IF a category window opens (new tech shift, new regulation) THEN the first mover with a funded POV engine has the advantage — but only if education budget is secured (positioning.md, T2).
- IF measurement is pipeline-based THEN switch to name-adoption metrics or the program will be killed on the wrong scoreboard (existing skill, T2).

## Failure modes
- "The next [leader]" positioning/creation — guaranteed failure (Kellogg) (T1).
- Running a "new category" campaign for a product that fits an existing category — wasted education budget (existing skill; Kellogg) (T2).
- Naming the category after your own product or internal jargon nobody adopts (existing skill) (T2).
- Teaching the market about the product instead of the problem — content collapses into a pitch (existing skill) (T2).
- Treating category design as a launch event instead of a multi-year adoption campaign (existing skill) (T2).
- Category-design economics claims are self-referential (authors sell the method) — mark as practitioner heuristic (positioning.md evidence evaluation) (T2).

## Contrarian beliefs
- Kellogg's anti-category-design position is the only practitioner actively attacking the field's biggest trend; treat it as the default skeptic before any category-creation budget (positioning.md) (T1).

## Conditions
Works in genuine whitespace with sustained funding, CEO evangelism, and analyst/ecosystem access (rarely correct for underfunded startups). Fails in crowded categories, without budget, or when the product only improves on existing criteria.

## Limitations
Evidence base is thin and self-referential (Play Bigger sells the method); category outcomes are influenced, not controlled; 76% economics figure is T3.
