## Beliefs
- Five Forces judges industry attractiveness and where value is captured — it is not a tool for profiling a company ("Apple has a strong brand" is about Apple; "buyer power is moderated because switching is costly" is about industry structure) (Investopedia; assignmenthelpcenter worked example) (T1).
- It is a diagnostic engine, not a checklist: "the model is being applied well when a team stops debating what's strong and starts asking what changes if this force shifts" (Visual-Paradigm) (T2).
- The most common mistake is drawing the industry boundary wrong — "we're in B2B SaaS" is not an industry; pin down the set of substitutes for this customer's job (DrinkBird) (T2).

## Principles
- Forces are interdependent feedback loops, not five independent boxes: a rise in buyer power can reduce rivalry; new entrants may exploit weak supplier power (Visual-Paradigm) (T2).
- The model is qualitative by design: numeric scoring + averaging = "the illusion of objectivity," it masks uncertainty (Visual-Paradigm) (T2).
- High force ≠ bad strategy: firms thrive in high-rivalry industries via position; industry attractiveness and company position are separate questions (Visual-Paradigm; existing skill) (T2).
- Static snapshots understate: read the direction of competitive pressure — which forces are strengthening, which the firm is working to weaken (assignmenthelpcenter) (T2).

## Frameworks
- Porter's Five Forces: rivalry, new entrants, supplier power, buyer power, substitutes (T1).
- Category lifecycle: emerging → growth → mature → declining (IBISWorld; existing skill); transitions show in entry/exit counts and pricing before revenue (T2 heuristic).
- Canonical tech profile (DrinkBird): SaaS — buyer power medium-low, supplier low-medium (cloud commodity, GPU dependence possible), entrants medium-high (low capital, trust takes time), rivalry high in commoditized categories, low in defended ones (T2).

## Processes
1. Define the industry precisely: buyer job + substitutability, geography, segment; state the scope and justify it (assignmenthelpcenter; DrinkBird) (T2).
2. Enumerate players by force, including substitutes outside the industry: LLMs, in-house builds, behavioral change, "do nothing" (DrinkBird; existing skill) (T2).
3. Score each force high/medium/low with named evidence — evidence, not instinct (Visual-Paradigm) (T2).
4. Show force interactions and trajectory (what would shift each force) (T2).
5. Judge overall attractiveness; separate industry attractiveness from your position in it (existing skill) (T2).
6. Pair with PESTLE (macro) and SWOT (firm) as complementary lenses (assignmenthelpcenter) (T2).
7. Re-run on force-shifting events: new tech, regulation, capital availability changes (DrinkBird) (T2).

## Heuristics
- "70% of Five Forces reports audited" show the same 6 mistakes (Visual-Paradigm — self-reported audit figure, T3) (T3).
- Substitutes are the most commonly missed force — the most dangerous substitutes look nothing like you (existing skill; DrinkBird) (T2).
- In regulated industries, player definitions may require legal input before scoping closes (Infomineo cross-reference) (T2).

## Inputs
Industry definition, competitor/player list, market data, regulatory sources, evidence of actual entry/switching behavior.

## Outputs
Force scores with evidence, lifecycle stage diagnosis, economics + profit-pool summary, regulatory scan, attractiveness verdict, strategic moves.

## Metrics
% forces with named evidence; force-shift events tracked; analysis age (re-run cadence met); stage-signal table completeness.

## Decision rules
- IF the analysis describes the company rather than the industry THEN restart with the industry as the unit (Investopedia, T1).
- IF the boundary is a broad label ("B2B SaaS") THEN re-draw it around substitutes for the customer's job (DrinkBird, T2).
- IF a force is scored without evidence THEN treat the score as labeling, not analysis (Visual-Paradigm, T2).
- IF forces are numerically averaged into a score THEN discard the average; keep qualitative judgment with interaction notes (Visual-Paradigm, T2).
- IF the market is converging or regulation is shifting THEN re-run annually or on force-shifting events (DrinkBird/Infomineo, T2).
- IF all five forces read "high" or all "low" THEN suspect boundary error or generic analysis — differentiate the forces (DrinkBird pattern, T2).

## Failure modes
- Analyzing the company, not the industry (most common misuse) (Investopedia) (T1).
- Ratings with no justification; industry defined too broadly; treating all forces as equal; no overall conclusion (assignmenthelpcenter) (T2).
- Ignoring substitutes and "do nothing" (existing skill) (T2).
- Static analysis: two-year-old industry brief misleads (existing skill; DrinkBird) (T2).
- Textbook-conclusion matching instead of market reality ("the conclusion matches a textbook example, not the market reality") (Visual-Paradigm) (T2).

## Contrarian beliefs
- Coffee-shop entry barriers are HIGH, not low, when digital delivery entrants with low fixed costs are counted (Visual-Paradigm example — intuition is often wrong) (T2).

## Conditions
Works on correctly bounded industries with observable structure; acknowledged weaknesses in fast-converging markets and platform ecosystems where complementors (not just substitutes) matter — note them rather than ignore (assignmenthelpcenter) (T2).

## Limitations
Snapshot bias; company-position blind spot; no complementor force in the original model; qualitative scoring invites labeling without discipline.
