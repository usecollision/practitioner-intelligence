## Beliefs
- Most market segmentation is wrong: "The fact that you're 18 to 35 years old with a college degree does not cause you to buy a product. It may be correlated with the decision, but it doesn't cause it" (OPINION, T1 — HBS Working Knowledge). JTBD seeks the causal mechanism of purchase, not correlations.
- Customers don't conform to segments; they take life as it comes and "hire" products to do jobs (FRAMEWORK, T1 — HBS).
- "Looking at the market from the function of a product really originates from your competitors or your own employees deciding what you need" — function-based views are supplier-centric (OPINION, T1 — HBS).

## Principles
- Crawl into the skin of your customer and go with her through her day, always asking "why did she do it that way?" (PRINCIPLE, T1 — HBS).
- Segment by job to be done, not product category or demographics (PRINCIPLE, T1).
- Purpose branding: build the brand around the job ("named after the purpose it serves") (PRINCIPLE, T1 — HBS).

## Frameworks
- Jobs to be Done (theory): causal mechanism of purchase = a job arises in the customer's life; products are hired to do it; competition for a job includes any substitute that can do the job (FRAMEWORK, T1 — HBS).
- "Integrating Around the Job to be Done" (teaching note) — organizing product/org around jobs (FRAMEWORK, T2).
- Functional/social/emotional dimensions of jobs (from Competing Against Luck) (FRAMEWORK, T2 — book canon; not in the HBS piece).

## Processes
- The milkshake study method: (1) observe the context of purchase (documented who bought, when, where consumed — a full day in the restaurant); (2) interview buyers in the moment of purchase about the job; (3) identify the job and the constraints; (4) design to the job (thicker milkshake for commuters, thinner for kids' treat) (PROCESS, T1 — HBS).
- Note the mix: observation first, then in-context interviews (PROCESS, T1).

## Heuristics
- If you understand the job, competitors outside your category are your real competitors (milkshake vs bagel/doughnut) (HEURISTIC, T1 — HBS).
- "I've got a list of mistakes that God made... he only made [so many] people" — all existing data is organized by product/customer category because that's how data gets collected; that misorganization makes you believe the market is organized that way (HEURISTIC, T1 — HBS).
- ~30,000 new consumer products launch each year and many fail (FACT, T1 — HBS).

## Tactics
- Observe before asking: document actual purchase behavior (who, when, where, to-go or dine-in) (TACTIC, T1).
- Ask about the job at the moment of purchase, not later (TACTIC, T1).
- Design different variants for different jobs in the same product line (commuter vs kid-treat milkshake) (TACTIC, T1).

## Tools
- HBS case materials; Competing Against Luck; the milkshake teaching note (TOOL, T1).

## Inputs
- Observation of purchase context; access to buyers at the moment of purchase; willingness to ignore demographic data (INPUT, T1).

## Outputs
- Job-based market segmentations; product design directives; purpose-branding strategy (OUTPUT, T1).

## Metrics
- Purchase/sales outcomes tied to job-specific variants; ability to articulate the job for each purchase (METRIC, HEURISTIC, T2).

## Decision rules
- When demographic or product-category segmentation hasn't moved sales, re-segment by job (DECISION RULE, T1 — milkshake case).
- If your data is organized by category/demographics, treat it as suspect for innovation decisions; collect job data yourself (DECISION RULE, T1 — HBS).

## Failure modes
- Asking customers to list ideal product characteristics (the failed first attempt in the milkshake case — "thick, thin, chunky...") — customers answered honestly and sales didn't move (FAILURE, T1 — HBS). This is the canonical documented failure of feature-request research.
- Segmenting by demographics or product category (FAILURE, T1).

## Contrarian beliefs
- Correlation (demographics) is not causation; most market research is built on correlation masquerading as insight (OPINION, T1).
- Feature/attribute feedback is near-useless without the job frame (OPINION, T1 — milkshake case).

## Examples
- Milkshake case: 40% of shakes sold in the morning to commuters with one free hand; job = make a boring commute more interesting + stave off hunger until noon; bagel/doughnut were the real competitors (EXAMPLE, T1 — HBS).
- Purpose branding: brand named after the purpose it serves (EXAMPLE, T2 — HBS mentions the concept; specific brand examples in his teaching notes).

## Conditions
- Works wherever a recurring purchase context exists; strongest for physical/consumer products and observable behavior (CONDITION, T1 — method built from observation).

## Limitations
- The method's observation-heavy approach is harder for digital B2B where the "moment of purchase" is diffused across a buying committee (LIMITATION, T2).
- JTBD explains switching, not market size or pricing (LIMITATION, T2).
